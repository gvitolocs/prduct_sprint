# Workstream D — the independent adviser's read

Furniture path only. Prepared 28 September 2026. Read as one body of material a furniture company would receive: the live tool, Round 1, and workstreams A, B and C. Markers: **[C]** confirmed against a primary text I opened today · **[I]** indicative · **[?]** unverified.

## 0. What I checked and what I could not

Opened today: Regulation (EU) 2025/2650 (Art. 2(15), (15b), (17); Art. 5; Art. 38; recitals 5–7); the ESPR (Art. 4(4), 7(5), 9(2)(d), 10(4), Annex III); the Commission's furniture DPP content draft of 22 September 2026 (§2.1.3.1, §7.4, §8.3, §11.2.6, Table 9-2); the Commission DPP timeline page; DI Produktion's March 2026 briefing; the Dansk Standard landing page for DS Hæfte 67:2026. EUR-Lex HTML was unreachable; publications.europa.eu served the texts. Not opened: the DS guide itself, Omnibus I, REACH entry 77, the EN standards.

The short version: A's regulatory reading is better than most vendor material I see, and honest where it costs Prduct. C is the cleanest set. B is nearly there. The original tool would not survive a second opinion. What remains wrong is not fabrication but **sizing**: EUDR is sized for the importer and applied to everyone; the DPP is sized for the item-level company and set as the top rung. Both sizes happen to be the vendor's.

## 2. Verdicts on the three sets

**A's field set.** Built with the right degree of caution in the prose and the wrong degree in the markers. A quotes the study's own caveat, tiers the rows, marks carbon footprint [?] and the wood rows as EUDR data first. That is more careful than the Commission's own summary pages. Two things fail. The [C] markers on tier A rows (finding 3) will be read as "confirmed for furniture" by exactly the reader who matters. And A's COPY suggestion to put all tiers on the result screen turns a sales page into a regulatory annex and sneaks the service-provider reference in as a requirement. One correction of fact: A's copy line "Timber origin is … not a passport field" overstates the other way; Table 9-2 carries "harvest origin for wood, mandatory where applicable, restricted" **[I]**, and A's own second sentence has it right. A's §3 EUDR-versus-DPP table is the honest "one data layer" argument and should be kept: it says what EUDR needs that a passport does not (customer identities, plot geolocation in substance, risk assessment, mass in kilograms) and the reverse. Its one gap is that for a downstream operator the EUDR column collapses to three items, which is finding 2.

**B's set.** Reads like a purchasing director's week: scorecard, framework agreement, onboarding pack, contract makers, substitutions, expiring certificates, customer's own material. The "(it depends)" rungs are the best design idea in the run and should be copied into the core set. Three questions manufacture exposure rather than report it: the fictional rule (6), the full material declaration (7) and the CoC penalty (11). The commercial argument holds precisely because B refuses to inflate it: "less than the compliance pitch implies", a PO-terms edit that costs nothing, a spreadsheet of expiry dates covering December. That candour is what a purchasing head will repeat to a colleague. The EUDR prompt needs the role fix (2).

**C's set.** The cleanest of the three. No regulatory dates, soft rungs that say "not a gap" for consumer brands, top rungs that are a maintained datasheet and a certificate list rather than a platform, and a commercial argument that declines to promise win rate and marks tender frequency [?]. `sales.landing` and `sales.artefact` are the two questions I would keep if only two survived. The only adviser's objection is finding 10, and C already lists the offer panel as AFTER-FRIDAY.

## 3. The second opinion

If a furniture company showed me this result screen with the two new modules, I would tell them to ignore: the score, the month band, the six rows as they stand, anything carrying "2029", and every top rung that says "item", "verified" or "connected". I would tell them to read the lifecycle diagram and the echoed facts carefully, because those are their own words about their own company and they are usually right.

Do first, without buying anything: answer the import question, which decides whether EUDR on 30 December is a filing duty or a filing-cabinet duty; list the wooden lines by CN code; classify each wood and panel supplier as operator, downstream operator or trader and ask what they will send; put a documentation line into standard PO terms at the next revision; make a one-page certificate register with expiry dates; and keep one versioned bill of materials per model at component level, in the ERP or in a spreadsheet, because everything in the Commission's draft table hangs on it. If they have a trade association, feed the draft back by 3 November **[C]**.

Buy something this quarter only if they are an EUDR operator with more consignments than a spreadsheet can carry, or if sales is answering questionnaires weekly. In both cases Prduct is a candidate, not the answer, and the diagnostic should say so.

DS Hæfte 67 says begin now, map your data and assess IT needs; DI's March briefing says the passport makes it "endnu vigtigere … at kunne opsamle og dokumentere sine produkters data" and points companies to its Data For Sustainability project, not to software. Neither names a furniture date or a granularity. Nothing in A, B or C contradicts them once findings 1–3 are fixed; the original tool's date and band do.

## 4. Where the two interests genuinely diverge

Most of this is aligned: a vendor selling to compliance professionals through NTT DATA into a market the size of Denmark is better served by a diagnostic that survives this read than by one that maximises urgency, and the Commission draft now makes "model is fine, batch where origin varies" the safe public position.

Three places are not aligned, and the trade-off should be named rather than smoothed. First, the role question (finding 2) will tell perhaps half of Danish respondents that their EUDR job is a spreadsheet; Prduct sells EUDR. The gain is that the other half, the importers, hear a real, dated, enforced obligation from a vendor who did not oversell it to their neighbour. Second, the number (finding 1): the funnel wants one, the respondent is better without one; a count of fillable fields is the compromise, and it is AFTER-FRIDAY. Third, the tier A rows (finding 3): Prduct's Art. 15 story is real and recurring, and it belongs in the deck, not on a screen labelled readiness.

## 5. Overall verdict

The three new workstreams would survive an independent read with the corrections above; A and C largely as written. The material is unusually willing to say "you probably do not need this yet". The original tool would not survive, and Round 1 has said why. The remaining risk is sizing, not invention, and the two-day fixes (role question, top rungs, markers, band) remove most of it.

## Where I limited myself

- **DS Hæfte 67 not read.** I reached the download landing page and its summary, not the PDF. The guide may say something about granularity or timing that would sharpen finding 4; a person with a browser can download it free and check in fifteen minutes.
- **DI checked through one briefing.** The March 2026 "Hvad, hvem og hvornår" piece only. DI's Data For Sustainability material and any furniture-specific DI or Lifestyle & Design Cluster guidance were not opened.
- **Omnibus I thresholds not verified.** The brief's premise that most mid-market furniture brands sit outside direct CSRD and CSDDD scope is taken as given **[I]**; the tool and the three workstreams do not invoke either directive, so nothing turned on it.
- **REACH Art. 33, entry 77 and the Textile Labelling Regulation** are cited from Round 1 and the Commission study's summaries, not the amending texts. Finding 7's list of what suppliers owe should be checked by someone who works with REACH daily before it becomes question text.
- **EUDR check percentages and the SME definition** were not re-read; I relied on A. The operator/downstream operator/trader analysis in finding 2 is from the amended Arts. 2 and 5 and recitals 5–7, which I did read.
- **No furniture company was asked.** Every judgement about how a purchasing or sales director reads B and C is mine. The AFTER-FRIDAY validation items in B and C are the fix.
- **CN-code edge cases.** Whether a metal-framed chair with wooden armrests falls under "ex 9401 seats of wood" is a customs question I did not pursue; Task 1 of the MEErP report holds the boundary cases per A.
- **Fillable-field count not specified.** Finding 1's replacement needs a data model tying answers to Table 9-2 rows; A's AFTER-FRIDAY item is the same work. I did not design it.
- **Battery precedent not used.** DIN DKE SPEC 99100 and Annex XIII would show what a mandatory field set actually looks like next to the furniture draft; out of scope by instruction.
